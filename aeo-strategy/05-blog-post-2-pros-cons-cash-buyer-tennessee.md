## A balanced take from someone in the industry

Most articles about "cash home buyers" are written by either (a) a cash-buying company trying to make themselves look good, or (b) a real estate agent trying to make cash buyers look bad. This one is written by a Tennessee cash buyer, but it's intended to be the post we'd send our own family member if they asked us "should I sell to a cash buyer?"

We'll cover the genuine advantages, the genuine disadvantages, and a Tennessee-specific framework for deciding which option is right for you.

## What you actually compare against

The realistic alternatives for selling a home in Tennessee in 2026 are:

1. **List with a real estate agent on the MLS** (the traditional path)
2. **For Sale By Owner (FSBO)** — list it yourself
3. **Cash sale to a buyer / investor** (what this article is about)
4. **iBuyer** (Opendoor was the major TN player; their model has shrunk a lot since 2022)

Most of this article will compare option 1 (agent listing) against option 3 (cash sale), since those are the two real choices for the vast majority of Tennessee homeowners.

## The pros — what a cash sale is genuinely good at

### 1. Speed (this is the big one)

A traditional MLS listing in Tennessee, in current market conditions, sits on the market 30–60 days on average before going under contract. Add 30–45 more days for the buyer's mortgage to close, and you're at 60–90+ days from list to keys-in-new-owner's-hands.

A cash sale can close in **as little as 7 days**, and almost always closes within 30. There's no lender, no appraisal, no inspection-driven repair negotiation, and no underwriting — the things that make a traditional sale slow are removed.

If you need certainty by a specific date — a job relocation, a foreclosure auction, a divorce decree, an estate settlement — a cash sale is often the only realistic option that meets the deadline.

### 2. No repairs

Tennessee retail buyers in 2026 expect homes to be in move-in condition or close to it. If your home has a 20-year-old roof, an HVAC system on borrowed time, foundation issues, water damage, or significant deferred maintenance, you face two choices when listing traditionally:

- **Make the repairs upfront.** This often costs $15,000–$60,000 and takes weeks or months.
- **List as-is and accept lower offers.** Buyers will discount the price more than the actual repair cost (because they're conservative and because their lender requires an appraisal).

A cash buyer absorbs all of this. We buy roofs we wouldn't sleep under and HVAC systems that don't turn on. The repair cost is built into our offer, so you don't have to write any checks before closing.

### 3. No agent commissions

In Tennessee, the typical real estate commission is **5–6% of the sale price** (split between the listing agent and the buyer's agent). On a $300,000 home, that's $15,000–$18,000 leaving your pocket at closing.

A cash sale has $0 in commissions. You're selling directly to the buyer.

(Note: the August 2024 NAR settlement changed how buyer-agent fees get negotiated, but in practice, most Tennessee transactions still see total commissions of 5–6% — buyers just negotiate their agent's share more explicitly now.)

### 4. No closing costs (typically)

In a traditional sale, sellers in Tennessee usually pay around **1–3%** in closing costs (title insurance for the buyer, transfer taxes, prorated property taxes, recording fees, sometimes seller-paid concessions to the buyer).

In a cash sale, most legitimate cash buyers cover all closing costs. The number we offer is the number you walk away with at closing — no "net sheet" surprise.

### 5. Certainty of sale

Roughly **3–5% of pending real estate sales nationally fall through** before closing — buyer financing falls apart, the appraisal comes in low, the inspection turns up something the buyer can't accept. In Tennessee's mid-tier markets, the rate is similar.

A cash sale has no lender, no appraisal, and an as-is contract. The only realistic ways it doesn't close are (a) a title problem (very rare), or (b) the seller backs out (always your option).

### 6. Privacy and no showings

For sellers who don't want strangers walking through their home twice a week — single mothers, elderly homeowners, those going through divorce, someone with the home contents still inside — a single private walkthrough beats 10–20 showings.

### 7. Buying with the home in any condition

Hoarding situations, code violations, inherited homes you haven't been to in years, properties with long-term tenants, properties in pre-foreclosure — these are all hard to list traditionally. Cash buyers buy them every day.

## The cons — what a cash sale is genuinely worse at

### 1. The offer will be below full retail

This is the most important thing to understand: **a cash buyer's offer is below what a fully renovated home would sell for on the open market**.

Why? The cash buyer is taking on:

- The cost of repairs and updates
- The cost of holding the property while it's being renovated (taxes, insurance, utilities, debt service)
- The cost of reselling it (marketing, agent commission on the resale, closing costs)
- The risk that something goes wrong (foundation surprise, market shift, carrying it longer than expected)
- A profit margin to stay in business

Realistic cash offers in Tennessee in 2026 typically come in at **70%–85% of the after-repair value (ARV)**, depending on how much work the house needs. For a home that doesn't need much work, the offer might be near the top of that range. For a major rehab, it'll be near the bottom.

Translation: if your fully-fixed-up home would sell for $300,000 retail, expect a cash offer in the range of $210,000–$255,000 minus repair costs, depending on the buyer's projection.

**Compared to a traditional listing**, after agent commissions, closing costs, and any required repairs, you typically net **$240,000–$275,000** on that same home — assuming you have the time, money, and patience to do it.

### 2. Less competition for your home

In a hot Tennessee market, listing on the MLS exposes your home to thousands of potential buyers. You might get multiple offers. A cash sale is a one-buyer negotiation, with no auction dynamic to push the price up.

### 3. You have to vet the buyer carefully

The cash-buying industry in Tennessee includes legitimate, licensed, well-capitalized buyers — and it includes "wholesalers" who don't actually have the cash, who lock sellers into contracts then try to assign them to a real buyer. If the assignment falls through, the seller is stuck.

Vetting tips:
- Look for a **BBB profile**. Not every cash buyer is BBB Accredited, but having an open profile with a rating tells you something.
- Look for a **named, photographed team** with real bios.
- **Demand the closing happen at a licensed Tennessee title company.** Period. No exceptions.
- Check that the offer isn't "subject to inspection" or "subject to assignment." A real cash buyer commits to a fixed number after the walkthrough.

### 4. Not every cash buyer is local

Tennessee has hundreds of "we buy houses" operations, and many are national lead-aggregators that resell your information to whichever local investor pays the most. The result: your phone rings off the hook, you talk to four different people, and the local buyer who actually closes the deal pays a lead fee that gets passed back to you in the form of a slightly lower offer.

You can avoid this by choosing a **locally owned Tennessee buyer** (not a national franchise or a lead aggregator). Tennessee Cash For Homes is one option — there are others.

## A simple framework for deciding

Ask yourself, in order:

1. **Do I have a hard deadline?** (Foreclosure, divorce, relocation, estate settlement.) If yes → cash sale is probably the right move.
2. **Does the home need significant repairs I don't want to handle?** If yes → cash sale, or traditional listing with a heavy as-is discount.
3. **Do I have the time, money, and energy to manage a 60–90 day MLS listing process?** If no → cash sale.
4. **Is maximizing the absolute highest price more important than anything else?** If yes → traditional listing with a Tennessee agent.

There's no universal right answer. The cash sale is a tool. It's the right tool for some situations and the wrong tool for others.

## What to ask any Tennessee cash buyer before signing

If you decide to talk to a cash buyer, here are the questions that separate the legitimate operators from the wholesalers:

1. **"Are you the actual buyer, or are you assigning the contract?"** A legitimate buyer says: *we're the buyer.*
2. **"What Tennessee title company will we close at?"** A legitimate buyer names one.
3. **"What's your earnest money deposit?"** A legitimate buyer puts $1,000+ into escrow at signing. Lower than that is a red flag.
4. **"Can I see your BBB profile and reviews?"** A legitimate buyer says yes immediately.
5. **"Is your offer subject to a future inspection?"** The right answer is *no, the price is firm after the walkthrough.*
