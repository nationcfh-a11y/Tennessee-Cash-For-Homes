Understanding the Inheritance Process in Tennessee

When someone passes away and leaves behind real estate, the home typically goes through probate. This is a court-supervised process that validates the deceased person’s will and distributes their estate. If the home was willed to you and you are the legal heir, the court will eventually transfer the title to your name. However, inheriting the title does not mean the mortgage disappears. Tennessee is not a community property state, but it does honor the concept of "due-on-sale" clauses under federal law exceptions. One such example is the Garn-St. Germain Act, which allows heirs to assume a mortgage without triggering a default.

This means you may take over the payments even if the loan technically remains in the deceased person’s name. But you are not off the hook. You are responsible for ensuring that payments are made or you could risk foreclosure.

Does the Mortgage Lender Need to Be Notified?

Yes, once you’ve inherited a property, you must notify the mortgage lender. Lenders have the legal right to know who is now responsible for the property. In Tennessee, mortgage companies typically work with heirs as long as payments continue. You might be offered options like loan assumption or even refinancing under your name, especially if the loan is federally backed by institutions such as FHA, VA, or USDA.

If the mortgage was private or subprime, however, the process might be more complicated. The lender may or may not allow assumption, and failing to notify them can trigger penalties or even foreclosure.

What Types of Mortgages Might Be Involved?

There are several kinds of mortgages you might inherit.

Conventional mortgages are the most common and typically involve fixed monthly payments. If you can’t keep up with the payments, selling the property may be your best option.

Reverse mortgages require immediate attention. These loans are due upon the borrower’s death, meaning you’ll need to pay off the loan or sell the home quickly.

Home equity lines of credit, also known as HELOCs, can complicate things. In some cases, the lender may call the loan due in full upon the borrower’s death. You’ll need to settle this balance as part of managing the estate.

Understanding which kind of loan is attached to the home is crucial in deciding whether to keep, rent, or sell the property.

What Are Your Options After Inheriting a Mortgaged Home?

Once you’ve inherited a property with a mortgage, you have a few main paths forward.

You can keep the home and continue making mortgage payments. This might make sense if you intend to live in the home or use it as a rental investment. However, this requires a solid financial plan, as you'll be assuming ongoing costs including taxes, insurance, and maintenance.

You can rent out the home to generate income that covers the mortgage. In a growing rental market like Nashville or Chattanooga, this might seem like a profitable idea. But being a landlord isn’t for everyone, especially if you live out of state.

You can sell the property, which is often the most straightforward solution, especially if the mortgage balance is significant. What if the Mortgage Is Underwater?

If the mortgage balance is higher than the home’s value, this is known as an underwater mortgage. In this case, you could consider a short sale with the lender’s permission, where the property is sold for less than the amount owed. Tennessee law requires lender approval for any short sale, and the process can be lengthy.

Alternatively, you can choose to decline the inheritance. While this may sound surprising, it is entirely legal. Under Tennessee law, if you file a formal disclaimer, you won’t be liable for the debt. The property will then pass to the next heir or revert to the estate.

How Inheritance Affects Property Taxes in Tennessee

Tennessee does not have a state inheritance tax as of 2016, and there is no estate tax either. However, you will still be responsible for paying annual property taxes as the new homeowner. Tax assessments may change if the property transfers ownership, so it's a good idea to contact your county assessor’s office to confirm the new tax basis.

If you're selling the inherited property, be mindful of capital gains taxes. The IRS uses a "stepped-up basis," meaning your cost basis is the market value of the property at the date of the previous owner's death. If you sell shortly after inheriting, you may owe little to no capital gains tax.
