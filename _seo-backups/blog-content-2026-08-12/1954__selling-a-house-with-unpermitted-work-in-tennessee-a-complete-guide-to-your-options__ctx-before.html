What is Considered Unpermitted Work in Tennessee?

Unpermitted work refers to any renovations, additions, or modifications to a property that were completed without obtaining the necessary approvals from the local building department or code enforcement authority. In Tennessee, permits are generally required for structural changes, electrical rewiring, plumbing upgrades, HVAC installations, and significant remodels. For example, if you finished a basement, converted a garage into a living space, added a deck, or moved walls inside your home without pulling the required permits, those projects are considered unpermitted. The rules vary by county and municipality, but the underlying purpose of permits is to ensure that work meets safety standards and complies with local building codes. Without the proper permits and inspections, there’s no official record that the work was done correctly, which is why it becomes a concern during the sale process. Many homeowners assume that because the work was done years ago without incident, it’s not an issue. Unfortunately, when you sell especially to a buyer using financing, unpermitted work can come under intense scrutiny.

The Risks of Selling a Home with Unpermitted Work

Selling a property with unpermitted work can introduce a range of challenges. First, there’s the legal risk. In Tennessee, if a buyer later discovers undisclosed unpermitted work, they could pursue legal action for misrepresentation or nondisclosure. Second, unpermitted work can make it difficult to pass inspection. Licensed inspectors are trained to identify renovations that may not have gone through the proper channels, and they will note these in their reports. Once an inspector flags an unpermitted addition or modification, buyers often become hesitant, worrying about safety, potential code violations, and future resale challenges.

Financing is another significant hurdle. Lenders generally require that all major work be properly permitted and inspected, especially if it impacts the structure, safety systems, or square footage of the home. If a lender won’t approve a mortgage due to unpermitted work, your pool of potential buyers shrinks dramatically. Even cash buyers in the open market may use the unpermitted status as a bargaining chip to negotiate a much lower price. These risks are why many homeowners in this situation turn to Tennessee Cash For Homes for a direct cash sale, eliminating the inspection and financing roadblocks entirely.

Tennessee Disclosure Laws and Unpermitted Work

Under Tennessee law, home sellers are required to disclose known material defects in the property. This includes unpermitted work if you are aware of it. The Tennessee Residential Property Disclosure form asks about improvements and repairs, and sellers must answer truthfully. Failing to disclose unpermitted work you know about can expose you to legal claims from the buyer after the sale. It’s important to note that you’re not required to actively investigate or search for unpermitted work you’re unaware of, but once you have knowledge — whether from your own involvement in the project or from an inspection report, you must disclose it. How Unpermitted Work Affects Traditional Home Sales

When you list your home on the open market through a real estate agent, buyers expect the property to meet certain legal and safety standards. If an inspector discovers unpermitted work, buyers may request that you either bring the work up to code, obtain retroactive permits, or significantly reduce your asking price. Obtaining retroactive permits can be a lengthy and costly process. It may require you to hire licensed contractors to open up walls, redo wiring or plumbing, and schedule multiple inspections. In some cases, local authorities may even require you to remove or demolish the unpermitted work entirely.

For example, if you added a bathroom without a permit and it doesn’t meet code, you may have to rip out fixtures and start over. This not only delays the sale but also adds unexpected expenses. Traditional buyers using mortgage financing may walk away completely if the unpermitted work impacts the appraised value or violates lender requirements.
