Understanding How a Reverse Mortgage Affects the Sale of a Home

A reverse mortgage, most commonly a Home Equity Conversion Mortgage or HECM, allows homeowners aged 62 and older to borrow against the equity in their home. Unlike a traditional mortgage, no monthly payments are required, and the loan balance grows over time as interest and fees accumulate. When the home is sold, the entire loan balance must be repaid in full. That balance often includes the original amount borrowed, accrued interest, mortgage insurance premiums, and servicing fees. In Tennessee, the lender holds a lien on the property until the loan is satisfied, meaning the sale cannot close until the payoff is delivered to the loan servicer.

Why Heirs Face a Tight Timeline After the Borrower’s Death

When a Tennessee homeowner with a reverse mortgage passes away, the loan becomes due and payable. Heirs generally have six months from the date of death to repay the loan, either by selling the home, refinancing into a traditional mortgage, or paying the balance outright. If more time is needed, two 90-day extensions can be requested, but they are not guaranteed and must be supported by evidence of active marketing or pending sale. Miss the deadline, and the lender can begin foreclosure proceedings. What Happens When the Loan Balance Exceeds the Home’s Value

One of the most common concerns with reverse mortgages is that the loan balance has grown to equal or exceed the home’s current value. The good news is that HECM loans are non-recourse, meaning heirs and homeowners are never personally liable for more than the home is worth at the time of sale. If the home is underwater, heirs can satisfy the loan by selling the property for 95 percent of its appraised value, and the FHA insurance covers the rest. This protection only applies if the loan is repaid through sale, however, so understanding the appraisal and payoff process in Tennessee is essential before listing or accepting any offer.

Selling While the Borrower Is Still Living

Homeowners with a reverse mortgage are free to sell their property at any time. The most common reasons Tennessee homeowners decide to sell include moving into a smaller home, relocating closer to family, or transitioning into assisted living. The sale proceeds first pay off the reverse mortgage balance, with any remaining equity returned to the homeowner. Because reverse mortgage payoffs are calculated daily and can change between the offer and closing, timing matters.
