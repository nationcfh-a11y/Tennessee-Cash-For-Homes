Clear, specific answers are a good sign. Vague ones are not.

Understanding Who You Are Actually Signing With

Start with the most basic question, because it is the one sellers skip most often. Who is the buyer on this contract? You want the legal name of the person or company that will appear on the paperwork, not just the name on the yard sign or the postcard that landed in your mailbox. Ask whether that entity is the one closing, or whether it is a marketing company that passes deals along to someone else. Then ask the follow up that tells you the most: do you use your own funds? Just recently a homeowner called before an in person meeting and asked exactly that, along with whether earnest money is submitted and whether contracts are assigned. Those are the right instincts. A buyer using their own capital controls the timeline. A buyer who still needs to find money has to borrow that certainty from somewhere else.

Which Title Company Will Handle Your Closing?

A legitimate cash purchase in Tennessee closes at a title company or a closing attorney's office, with a title search, a settlement statement, and a recorded deed. Ask for the name of the specific company the buyer intends to use and whether you can call them directly. You are allowed to ask for a different closer if you have one you trust. The title company is also where problems surface, including liens, probate questions, and unclear ownership, so knowing who is handling that work tells you how seriously the buyer takes the back end of the deal. How Much Earnest Money Are You Putting Down?

Earnest money is the deposit a buyer places with the title company to show they are serious. Ask for the amount, when it will be delivered, and where it is being held. A deposit held by a neutral third party means more than one held by the buyer. The number itself matters less than the pattern: a buyer willing to put real money at risk is telling you something about their confidence in closing. A buyer who wants a contract signed with little or nothing on the line is keeping all of the flexibility and giving you none of it. How Long Is the Inspection Period?

Almost every cash contract includes a window for the buyer to inspect the property and confirm their numbers. Ask how many days that window runs and what the buyer can do during it. A short, defined period is a sign the buyer already knows what they are looking at. A long period, or one that can be extended repeatedly, keeps your house tied up while you wait. Also ask what happens to the price at the end of that window. Some buyers use the inspection period as an opportunity to renegotiate after you have already stopped talking to anyone else. You are entitled to ask directly whether the offer you signed is the number you will see on the settlement statement.

Can the Contract Be Assigned to Another Buyer?

Assignment means the buyer can transfer the contract to a different buyer, who then closes in their place. It is a common and legal practice in real estate, and it is not automatically a problem, but you deserve to know in advance. Ask whether the contract contains an assignment clause, whether the buyer intends to use it, and who would actually show up at closing if they do. The answer changes what you are relying on. If the contract can be assigned, you are depending on the buyer's ability to find someone else, not on their own funds. What Happens If You Back Out Before Closing?

Ask the uncomfortable question out loud. If the buyer cancels after the inspection period, what do you keep? Does the earnest money become yours, or is it refundable in most situations? How much notice will you receive? Then ask how often it has happened to them and what they did about it. The answer you want is a straight one, not a promise that it never happens. Every contract defines these outcomes differently, so read the cancellation and default language before you sign and have a title company or a real estate attorney explain anything that is unclear. The cost of that review is small compared to the cost of a deal that collapses two days before your move.

What Does Their Track Record in Middle Tennessee Look Like?

The first thing we recommend to any seller is to call around and get quotes from several companies. Talking to three buyers teaches you more about your house, and about the people making offers, than reading a hundred reviews. Ask each one how long they have been buying in your area, whether they buy in your county, and whether they can point you to recent closings. Look at their reviews. Look at whether they answer the phone. Know the Trade Off: Price for Speed and Convenience

One honest answer separates good buyers from the rest, and it is rarely the one sellers want to hear. When you sell to a cash home buyer, you are selling at a discount. You are trading price for speed and convenience: no repairs, no showings, no commissions, and a closing date you choose. A buyer who sets that expectation up front is being straight with you. A buyer who claims you will get full retail value and a seven day close is managing you, not informing you. Decide which side of that trade matters more in your situation before you sign, because the right answer depends entirely on what you need. If maximizing the number is the priority, listing may serve you better, and understanding how a cash offer is calculated will help you judge whether an offer is reasonable.

Final Thoughts on Questions to Ask a Cash Home Buyer in Tennessee

Interview the person you decide to work with. Make sure you trust them, that they have strong reviews and a real track record, and that you have genuine confidence they can perform on the agreement you sign. Ask who the buyer is, whether the funds are theirs, which title company closes, how much earnest money is going down, how long the inspection period lasts, whether the contract can be assigned, and what happens if they walk. Write the answers down and compare them side by side. Contract terms and legal requirements vary by situation, so have a Tennessee title company or attorney review anything you do not fully understand before signing.
