Understanding How a Guardian or Conservator Sale Works in Tennessee

A Tennessee court appoints a guardian when a minor needs someone to manage property, and a conservator when an adult has been found unable to manage their own affairs. Neither appointment transfers ownership. The home still belongs to the protected person, and the fiduciary simply holds authority to act within the limits written into the appointment order.

Real estate sits at the top of the list of assets courts watch closely, since the house is usually the largest thing the protected person owns and the proceeds can be spent or lost once it sells. Tennessee practice generally requires court permission before a fiduciary transfers real property, even when selling is clearly the right call. Your attorney should confirm what your specific order allows, because the powers granted vary from case to case.

Guardian, Conservator, and Power of Attorney Are Not the Same

This distinction causes more failed closings than almost anything else. A power of attorney is a private document signed by the owner while they still had capacity, with no court involved. A conservatorship is a court proceeding opened after capacity is gone or disputed, and it typically supersedes an earlier power of attorney for whatever the court takes over.

Families often arrive at closing holding a durable power of attorney signed years ago, unaware that a conservatorship was later opened. The title company will find it in the county records and stop relying on the power of attorney. Selling with the wrong instrument creates a defect that follows the property, and cleaning that up later costs far more than pausing now. Why the Court Order Matters More Than the Contract

In an ordinary sale the purchase agreement drives everything. In a guardianship or conservatorship sale the court order does, and a contract signed by a fiduciary who lacks authority is not a reliable path to closing no matter how fair the price is.

Underwriters generally look for an order that identifies the property clearly, names the fiduciary permitted to sign, and states that the sale is approved. Orders saying only that the conservator has general authority over the estate invite follow up questions, because the examiner cannot tell whether real property was included. Describe the property by address and by deed book and page in your petition, and ask the court to authorize execution of the deed and all closing documents.

What Middle Tennessee Title Companies Usually Ask For

Expect the closing attorney to request certified copies rather than the photocopies in your folder. A typical file includes current letters of guardianship or conservatorship, the order authorizing the sale, proof that notice went to interested parties, and confirmation that the fiduciary bond, if one was ordered, is adequate for the sale proceeds.

Underwriters also want to confirm the fiduciary has not been removed or replaced since the appointment. Request certified copies early, because clerks in Davidson, Williamson, and Sumner counties each work on their own timelines and a copy request can quietly eat a week. Ask the closing attorney for their checklist before you go to court, not after.

Three Ways to Structure a Cash Sale Around Court Approval

Three structures show up repeatedly. The cleanest is a fully court authorized sale: the fiduciary petitions first, obtains the order, then signs a contract matching the approved terms. Nothing is contingent, and closing proceeds like any other cash purchase.

The second is a contract made expressly contingent on court approval, with earnest money in escrow and the closing date keyed to entry of the order rather than a fixed calendar date. That lets you line up a buyer while the petition is pending, which matters when a vacant home is stacking up carrying costs.

The third is a proceeds holdback, where part of the money stays in escrow after closing until a condition is met. Holdbacks work well for repairs and lien payoffs. Most underwriters will not insure around a gap in the fiduciary's authority, so treat court approval as something to obtain, not something to paper over.

Sample Language That Keeps a Closing on Schedule

The wording below is a starting point for a conversation with your attorney, not a form to file. Tennessee judges and local practice vary, and your counsel should adapt anything you use.

For the petition: "Petitioner, as duly appointed conservator, requests authority to sell the real property located at [address], described in the deed of record at Book [number], Page [number], [county] Register of Deeds, and to execute the deed and all documents necessary to complete the sale, with net proceeds deposited into the conservatorship account."

For the contract: "This agreement is contingent upon entry of an order authorizing Seller, in Seller's fiduciary capacity, to convey the property. Closing shall occur within ten business days after entry of that order. If no order is entered within [number] days, either party may terminate and Buyer's earnest money shall be refunded in full."

What Slows These Sales Down in the Nashville Area

Most delays come from four places: court calendars that do not have a hearing date when you need one, missing notice to interested parties, a bond sized for a modest estate that must be increased before the court will release real estate proceeds, and disagreement among family members.

None of these are reasons to avoid selling. They are reasons to start the court process before you start marketing the home. Fiduciaries who petition first and shop second routinely close in a fraction of the time, which matters when a house in Nashville or Murfreesboro sits empty while care costs climb.

Final Thoughts on Selling a Tennessee Home Under a Conservatorship

The legal layer is real, but it is manageable. Confirm which authority actually governs the property, get the court order before you commit to a closing date, assemble the certified documents the title company will ask for, and choose a buyer whose timeline can flex around a hearing.

Because guardianship and conservatorship rules in Tennessee turn on the language of your appointment order and on local court practice, work with a Tennessee attorney on the filings themselves.
