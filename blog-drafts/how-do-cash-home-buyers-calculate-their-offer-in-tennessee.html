One is what a finished house sells for after somebody spends months and tens of thousands of dollars getting it there. The other is what a buyer can pay today, as is, and still cover that work. Understanding How a Cash Offer Is Calculated in Tennessee

Every legitimate cash offer comes from the same four part formula. Start with the after repair value, which is what the house would sell for on the open market once it is fixed up. Subtract the cost of the repairs. Subtract the costs of owning and reselling the property, which the buyer pays and you never see. Subtract a profit margin. What remains is what the buyer can responsibly offer you.

You may hear this described as the 70 percent rule, meaning an investor pays about 70 percent of the after repair value minus repairs. It is shorthand rather than a law, and the percentage moves with the condition of the house and the local market. A Tennessee property that needs almost nothing supports a much higher percentage than one needing a roof, a heat and air system, and a kitchen. The formula stays the same either way, so once you understand the four inputs you can check any offer against them.

It Starts with After Repair Value, Not Your Zestimate

After repair value is the anchor for everything else, and it is determined the same way an appraiser would do it: by looking at what comparable homes near yours actually sold for recently, in similar condition, with similar square footage and bedroom counts. Not what they listed for, and not what an algorithm estimates.

This is usually where the conversation starts, because the number in your head often came from an online estimate. Those tools work from public records and averages, and they cannot see that your kitchen is original to 1978, that the back bedroom has water staining on the ceiling, or that your neighbor renovated before selling. Our post on why the Zestimate does not determine your home's value goes deeper, but the short version is that an automated estimate is a starting point for a conversation, not a price.

The Repair Estimate Is the Biggest Variable

Once the after repair value is set, the buyer walks the property and builds a repair budget: roof, foundation, heat and air, electrical, plumbing, windows, flooring, kitchen, baths, paint, and whatever the house is hiding. This is the number that swings an offer the most, and it is the one homeowners most often underestimate, usually because they are pricing the work they would do rather than the work a buyer has to do to sell at full retail.

There is a real difference between livable and market ready. You may be perfectly comfortable with the carpet and the old countertops, but a retail buyer with a mortgage and an inspector will not be, and the lender may not be either. A buyer also has to budget for what turns up after demolition starts, because in an older Tennessee home something always does. The Costs a Cash Buyer Carries That You Never See

This category is invisible to most sellers, and it is substantial. The buyer pays closing costs when they purchase from you, and in a cash sale they typically cover your side too. Then they own the house through the renovation, which means property taxes, insurance on a vacant property (which costs more than a normal policy), utilities, and the cost of the money itself, whether that is a loan or capital that could be earning elsewhere.

When the work is finished the house goes back on the market, and now there are agent commissions on the resale, another round of closing costs, and often concessions to the eventual buyer. These costs commonly run 10 to 15 percent of the after repair value. None of it shows up on your closing statement, but every dollar has to come out of the spread between what the buyer pays you and what the house eventually sells for.

The Profit Margin, and Why It Is Not a Secret

The last subtraction is profit. A company that buys houses is taking real risk: that the repairs run over, that the market softens during the four or five months it owns your house, that something structural turns up behind a wall. Without a margin that absorbs those risks, one bad project ends the business, and the buyer who promised to close on your house is gone.

Reputable Tennessee buyers are straightforward about this. The margin is a line in the math, not a secret, and you are entitled to ask what it is. What should concern you is not that a buyer earns a profit, it is a buyer who will not explain any part of how they got to their number.

A Tennessee Example, Start to Finish

Picture a three bedroom brick ranch in Rutherford County. Updated comparable homes nearby have been selling around $340,000, so that is the after repair value. The house needs a roof at roughly $12,000, a heat and air system at $8,000, a kitchen and two baths at $25,000, flooring and paint throughout at $15,000, and a $10,000 allowance for the surprises, which totals $70,000 in repairs.

Transaction and holding costs at about 12 percent of the after repair value come to roughly $40,000, covering both closings, five months of taxes, insurance, and utilities, the cost of capital, and the commission on the resale. Say the buyer needs $35,000 of profit to justify the risk. Now the math: $340,000 minus $70,000 minus $40,000 minus $35,000 leaves an offer near $195,000. That is not an insult aimed at your house. It is what is left after the work that stands between its condition today and that $340,000 comparable sale.

Why the Offer Looks Low Next to Your Neighbor's Sale Price

The honest comparison is not offer price against your neighbor's sale price. It is what you would net, in hand, at the end of each path. On the traditional route you might list that same house as is somewhere in the $250,000 range, then negotiate repair credits after the inspection, pay around 6 percent in commissions, cover your share of closing costs, and keep making the mortgage payment, the insurance premium, and the tax bill for however long it takes to close.

Run that out and the gap between the two paths is usually smaller than the headline numbers suggest, though a cash sale does typically net less. What you buy with the difference is certainty and speed: no repairs out of your pocket, no showings, no appraisal, no financing contingency that collapses two weeks before closing, and a date you choose. How to Tell a Fair Offer from a Lowball

Ask the buyer to show you their after repair value and which comparable sales it came from. Ask for their repair estimate broken into categories. A buyer who has genuinely evaluated your Tennessee property can answer both questions without hesitating, and you can sanity check the comps yourself in a few minutes online.

Then watch what happens after the offer. The warning sign in this industry is not a low number, it is a number that moves. A buyer who offers high to win the contract, then discovers problems during the inspection period and comes back asking for a $30,000 reduction the week of closing, has cost you a month and all your leverage. An offer that is a little lower and does not change is worth more than a high one that quietly renegotiates. Final Thoughts on How Cash Offers Are Calculated

A cash offer is not a judgment about your home or a guess pulled from the air. It is after repair value minus repairs, minus the costs of carrying and reselling, minus the margin that keeps the buyer solvent enough to actually close. Once you can see those four inputs, an offer stops feeling arbitrary, and you can evaluate it the way you would any other business proposition.

Get more than one offer, ask every buyer to walk you through their numbers, and compare what you would truly net on each path rather than comparing an as is offer to a renovated neighbor's sale price. Some Tennessee homeowners run that comparison and decide to list, which is the right call when the house shows well and time is not pressing. Others look at the repair list and the months involved and decide the certain number today is worth more than the theoretical one later.
