Understanding What a Tenant's Option to Buy Actually Is

An option to purchase gives your tenant the right, but not the obligation, to buy the property on agreed terms within an agreed window. You are bound. The tenant is not. That asymmetry is the whole point of an option, and it is why the wording matters so much.

An option is also different from a right of first refusal, and Tennessee landlords mix the two up constantly. An option lets the tenant trigger a sale on their own schedule at a set price. A right of first refusal only wakes up when you bring a third party offer to the table, at which point the tenant usually gets a defined period to match it. If your lease grants a right of first refusal rather than a true option, your path to a cash sale is typically much shorter. Pull the document and find out which one you have before you do anything else.

Reading Your Lease Option Clause Line by Line

Sit down with the full lease, every addendum, and any separate option agreement. You are looking for six things: the exercise deadline and whether it has passed, how the tenant must deliver notice of exercise (many clauses require written notice by certified mail), the purchase price or the formula that sets it, whether option consideration or rent credits have been accumulating, whether the option is conditioned on the tenant being current on rent, and whether the option binds successors or can be assigned to someone else.

That last point drives everything. Some clauses say plainly that the agreement binds successors and assigns. Others are silent. The exact language, not the general concept, determines what you can deliver at closing, so this is the moment to have a Tennessee real estate attorney read the clause with you.

Does an Unexercised Option Block a Cash Sale in Tennessee?

In most situations, no. You still own the property and you can still contract to sell it. What changes is the quality of what you are selling. If the option is live and your buyer knows about it, that buyer may be taking the property with the tenant's right attached. A tenant living in the home is a visible fact that puts any reasonable buyer on notice to ask questions, so assume a serious buyer will find the option whether or not anything was ever recorded.

Outcomes in disputed cases depend heavily on the wording and on how both sides have behaved since signing, and Tennessee courts have resolved option disputes in a range of ways. Whether the Option Shows Up on Your Title

Sometimes a tenant or their attorney records a memorandum of option with the county Register of Deeds. When that happens, the option appears in a title search and your closing attorney treats it like any other cloud that must be cleared before a clean transfer. If nothing was recorded, the option can still be enforceable between you and the tenant, it just will not appear in the public record.

Either way, the fix resembles clearing other encumbrances. First Steps to Take Before You Accept Any Offer

Assemble the complete file: the original lease, every renewal, all addenda, the payment history, and any email or text where the option was discussed. Then confirm the status of the exercise window. If the deadline passed and the tenant never gave proper notice, document that carefully and preserve your records.

Be deliberate about what comes next. Continuing to accept payments labeled as option consideration after expiration, or verbally agreeing to extend a deadline, can undercut the position you are trying to protect. Put every communication in writing, ask the tenant directly whether they intend to exercise, and give them a reasonable date to respond. Many Middle Tennessee tenants already know they cannot qualify for financing and will say so, which clears the path quickly.

How Cash Buyers Structure Closings Around an Option

Four structures come up most often. A negotiated release is the cleanest: the tenant signs a written release of the option for consideration, and that release is recorded if a memorandum was ever filed. Selling subject to the option means the cash buyer accepts the property with the tenant's right in place and the contract spells out who carries that risk.

An escrowed holdback keeps part of your proceeds in escrow until the option expires or is released, which lets the closing happen now instead of months from now. A conditional closing timed to the expiration date works when the deadline is close and both sides can wait a few weeks. Investors who handle creative terms are comfortable with all four, and the same thinking appears in our guides to owner financing and subject-to sales.

What Your Title Company Will Want to See

Expect your closing attorney or title company to ask for three things: the complete lease and option documents, proof of the option's current status (a signed release, evidence the window closed without proper notice, or a written exercise if the tenant is actually buying), and a tenant estoppel certificate confirming the lease terms, rent, deposit, and whether the tenant claims any purchase rights.

Lining up those three items before you go under contract is the single biggest time saver. It turns a deal that would stall for sixty days into one that closes on schedule.
