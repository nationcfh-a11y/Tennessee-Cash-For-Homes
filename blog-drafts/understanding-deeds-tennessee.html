What Is a Deed and Why It Matters

A deed is the written legal document that transfers ownership of real property from one party, called the grantor, to another, called the grantee. In Tennessee, a valid deed must identify both parties, describe the property, state that ownership is being conveyed, and be signed by the person giving up ownership. Without a properly prepared and recorded deed, a sale is not legally complete, no matter what a contract or verbal agreement says. Understanding which type of deed you are signing matters because each one carries a different level of protection and responsibility, and choosing the wrong one can leave you or the new owner exposed to title trouble down the road.

The Difference Between a Deed and the Title

People often use the words deed and title interchangeably, but they are not the same thing. Title is the legal concept of ownership itself, your bundle of rights to use, control, and sell the property. The deed is the physical document that transfers that title from one person to another. It helps to think of title as the ownership and the deed as the vehicle that moves it. When you sell a home in Tennessee, you use a deed to convey title to the buyer. This distinction matters because problems can cloud a title, such as unpaid liens or competing claims, even when the deed itself looks perfectly valid on paper.

General Warranty Deeds: The Strongest Protection

The general warranty deed is the most common deed used in standard Tennessee home sales, and it offers the buyer the greatest protection. With this deed, the seller guarantees that they hold clear title and have the right to sell, and they promise to defend the buyer against any title claims, even ones that arose before the seller owned the property. If a hidden lien or ownership dispute surfaces years later, the buyer has legal recourse against the seller. Because of these strong assurances, buyers and their lenders typically expect a general warranty deed in a traditional arm’s-length sale between unrelated parties in Tennessee.

Special Warranty Deeds: Limited Guarantees

A special warranty deed, sometimes called a limited warranty deed, offers a narrower promise. Here the seller guarantees clear title only for the period during which they owned the property, not for anything that happened before they took ownership. This type of deed is common when the seller is an estate, a bank selling a foreclosed home, or a company that wants to limit its long-term liability. For a buyer, a special warranty deed provides less protection than a general warranty deed, so it is important to understand exactly what is and is not being guaranteed before closing on a Tennessee property.

Quitclaim Deeds: Common Among Family Members

A quitclaim deed transfers whatever interest the grantor has in a property, but it makes no promises about the title at all. If the grantor owns the property free and clear, the grantee receives full ownership; if the grantor owns nothing, the grantee receives nothing. Because they carry no warranties, quitclaim deeds are rarely used in arm’s-length sales. Instead, Tennessee families use them to add or remove a spouse after marriage or divorce, transfer property between relatives, move a home into a trust, or clear up a minor title question. They are simple and fast, but a buyer should never accept one in place of a warranty deed in a normal purchase.

Other Deeds Tennessee Homeowners May Encounter

Beyond the three main types, several specialized deeds appear in Tennessee real estate. An executor’s or administrator’s deed is used when a property is sold out of an estate during probate, conveying the deceased owner’s interest to the buyer. A trustee’s deed appears in connection with a deed of trust, the instrument Tennessee uses instead of a traditional mortgage to secure home loans, and it is the deed delivered after a foreclosure sale. You may also see a survivorship deed, which controls how co-owners inherit one another’s share. How Deeds Are Signed and Recorded in Tennessee

For a deed to be effective in Tennessee, it must be in writing, signed by the grantor, and acknowledged before a notary public. Once signed, the deed should be recorded with the Register of Deeds in the county where the property sits. Recording is what puts the public on notice of the new ownership and protects the buyer’s claim against later disputes. Tennessee also charges a state transfer tax when a deed is recorded, calculated based on the property’s value and typically handled at closing.
